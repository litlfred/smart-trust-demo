Journeys J1–J4

Somsack is born in Thailand, grows up in Lao PDR, and one year later a Thai doctor can see every vaccine he has ever had, without a single record being copied across the border.

The story is the one the VaxLink workshop walkthrough tells (source), documented here as a WHO SMART Guidelines L2 DAK and L3 implementation guide. Its FHIR examples are materialized in this guide; the VaxLink examples page maps each moment and device screen to them.

  • Country A (birth country) is Thailand. Somsack is born at Chiang Rai Provincial Hospital and gets his birth doses there (J1). On the GDHCN DEV network, Thailand is played by the test participant XXR, whose DSC (kid kW/6VnwPRKI=, hex 916ffa567c0f44a2) is on the live DEV trust list.
  • Country B (country of residence, “home”) is Lao PDR. The family lives there (J2, J3, J4.0). On the DEV network it is played by a fictional participant, written XXH.
  • J4.1 takes the family back to Thailand, a year later.

XXR and XXH are DEV test participants, not either country’s production keys, and nothing here implies a real ministry. Every person is fictional.

The cast: Khamla SENGMANY (mother), Bounmy SENGMANY (father, holds the family phone), Vilay (community health worker, Village 04), Nurse Chanthasone (Vientiane District Health Center) and Dr Anan (Thai clinician, at the birth and a year later).

User scenarios

Each step names the requirements of smart-trust-tools it exercises, and the FHIR examples of this guide it produces. The stories are in scenarios/stories.json. Each moment ends with its pain point, what enables it, and the policy point it makes, as the VaxLink walkthrough presents them.

J1 — Born abroad (Thailand, birth day)

Precondition: the issuer’s DSC is on the trust list. XXR’s document signer certificate (DSC), issued under XXR’s Country Signing CA (CSCA), was shared with the GDHCN, the Trust Anchor, before any DVC is signed; the GDHCN publishes it as a DID document. Sharing it is not a step of issuing. A DVC signed with a key whose DSC was never shared fails every receiver’s trust-list check (STT-VER-006).

Khamla gives birth to Somsack on 2025-10-28 at Chiang Rai Provincial Hospital, away from home. He has no Thai identity number. Dr Anan gives the Hep B and BCG birth doses and records them with their WHO PreQual product codes. For each dose, the hospital system does three things:

  1. It builds a DVCMinPreQual payload: STT-PAY-001…005 and STT-PAY-007, with subclaim −6 (STT-SEL-003). No identity document is needed (STT-PAY-005).
  2. It prepares the CWT and the bytes to sign: STT-CWT-001…005, STT-ISS-004.
  3. It has XXR’s key sign them (STT-COS-002, STT-COS-006), then finishes the HC1 text: STT-COS-001, STT-ZIP-001, STT-B45-001, STT-HC1-001, STT-QR-001.

Khamla leaves with two paper DVCs. In the demo: the library tests use the WHO guide’s own two signed HC1 strings (QR Code Generation §12), which verify against the live DEV trust list under the same XXR key. Somsack’s DVC payloads are illustrative, as in VaxLink. Live issuance in the app uses a TEST-ONLY key (STT-ISS-003), whose DSC is not on the GDHCN list, so its DVCs do not verify in J3.

  • Pain point: The baby has no Thai ID number, so his records would normally stay locked inside the Thai hospital’s system.
  • Enabler: A digitally signed QR card travels with the family — no ID number or database lookup needed.
  • Policy: Babies born abroad can get a verifiable vaccine record before they have the host country’s civil registration or a national ID — and registering the birth at home still matters.

J2 — Found at home (Lao PDR, 3 weeks later)

J2a. On a routine visit to Village 04, Vilay finds a newborn the Lao system does not know exists. Her phone has had no signal for weeks. Khamla shows the two cards. The CHW app reads them with read-unverified (STT-VER-011; STT-B45-002, STT-ZIP-002, STT-CBR-003), which needs no trust list. It shows the doses, but checks no signature and saves nothing yet.

J2b. On the CHW app’s newborn form (structured data capture, TrustDemoHouseholdRegistration) she adds Somsack to the Sengmany household (TrustDemoHousehold), with a temporary id. Bounmy confirms the details as head of household. The app saves the doses read from the cards as reported by the family, not verified (TrustDemoJ2HepBReported, TrustDemoJ2BcgReported: primarySource false). Vilay refers Somsack to the district health center (TrustDemoJ2Referral). Everything stays queued on the phone, for 28 days if need be, and syncs when it next reaches a signal.

  • Pain point (J2a): Without a check, a forged or mistyped card could quietly become an official record.
  • Pain point (J2b): Babies born abroad are invisible to the home health system until civil registration catches up — sometimes months later.
  • Enabler (J2a): The phone reads the card offline, and keeps “what the family told us” clearly separate from “what we have verified”.
  • Enabler (J2b): An offline form links the baby to his household immediately; it syncs once the phone finds a signal.
  • Policy (J2a): Keep a clear line between what families report and what has been verified, and don’t grant trust too early.
  • Policy (J2b): Newborns can be registered at home, offline, before civil registration or a national ID exists.

J3 — Made official (Vientiane District Health Center, 4 weeks later)

Precondition: the DID documents are already loaded. Loading them is not a step of this visit. The clinic may be offline all day, so the EHR fetches nothing during it: its trust store holds the GDHCN DEV trust list as it was last refreshed, including XXR’s DSC DID document (STT-TRU-001, STT-TRU-002, STT-TRU-005). A clinic with no connection of its own refreshes it about weekly, when the CHW visits the base clinic or district health office, where Country B retrieves the list the GDHCN trust anchor distributes. A DSC published after that refresh is not in the clinic’s copy, so its DVC fails the trust-list check (STT-VER-006) until the next refresh.

J3a. Nurse Chanthasone opens the referral and scans the QRs. The EHR verifies each DVC offline against that copy (STT-VER-001…009, STT-KID-002), without contacting Thailand. Both verify, so it imports them as FHIR Immunizations (TrustDemoJ3HepB, TrustDemoJ3Bcg) with provenance (TrustDemoJ3Verification; STT-VER-007, STT-VER-008), replacing the reported doses of J2. If a DVC fails (a tampered card, say), the EHR names the step and the reason, the doses stay unverified, and the nurse decides by national catch-up policy.

J3b. The Thai Hep B product matches nothing on the national schedule. The EHR keeps Thailand’s product coding and adds the schedule coding beside it, from the national map (TrustDemoProductToLaoEpi). For the Hep B product the map has no entry, so Nurse Chanthasone makes the clinical call that it is the schedule’s birth dose (TrustDemoJ3Adjudication), and her decision is added to the map for the next clinic. The schedule (TrustDemoLaoInfantSchedule) then runs: the birth doses count, and pentavalent 1, bOPV 1 and PCV 1 are due at 6 weeks (TrustDemoJ3Recommendation). Khamla leaves with the date (TrustDemoJ3Reminder). Over the year Somsack gets those doses and measles-rubella at 9 months, in Lao PDR.

  • Pain point (J3a): Clinicians either distrust foreign paper and re-vaccinate, or trust it blindly.
  • Pain point (J3b): Countries name and package vaccines differently, so doses get missed or repeated.
  • Enabler (J3a): Thailand’s signing key is on the shared WHO trust list, so any clinic can check it locally.
  • Enabler (J3b): Thailand’s original code is kept and the Lao code is added alongside; the schedule engine calculates what’s due.
  • Policy (J3a): Trust is decentralized: a Lao clinic verifies a Thai signature itself, without contacting Thailand.
  • Policy (J3b): Never overwrite another country’s record: keep the original coding alongside the national one.

J4.0 — Crossing back: one family, one phone (Lao PDR, 12 months later)

J4.0a. The family plans a trip to Thailand. Bounmy’s phone holds the family wallet, with a profile for each of them. Somsack’s summary (TrustDemoJ4Ips) shows six doses: two from Thailand, four from Lao PDR.

Precondition, as in J1: Country B’s DSC, under Country B’s CSCA, is already shared with the GDHCN, the Trust Anchor. That is what lets the clinic in Thailand verify the link in J4.1.

J4.0b. For Somsack, Bounmy shares his record with a 4-digit passcode he can say aloud, valid for 14 days. The portal records his consent (TrustDemoJ4Consent), then does three things:

  1. It assembles the IPS and Somsack’s DVCs.
  2. It encrypts them (STT-VHL-004, STT-VHL-007) behind a passcode-protected manifest with an expiry (STT-VHL-009).
  3. It issues the link as an HCERT, with the VHL at key 5 (STT-VHL-001, STT-VHL-002), signed with Country B’s DSC.

The QR holds only a pointer and a key: the record stays in Lao PDR.

  • Pain point (J4.0a): Many families share one smartphone; one-app-per-person designs leave them out.
  • Pain point (J4.0b): Sending photos or PDFs of records leaks data and can’t be verified by the receiver.
  • Enabler (J4.0a): A household wallet on one phone, with a separate health summary for each person.
  • Enabler (J4.0b): A Verifiable Health Link: the QR holds only a locked pointer; the data stays home until the passcode is given.
  • Policy (J4.0a): Design for shared devices (“One Family, One Phone”) so families with only one phone aren’t left out.
  • Policy (J4.0b): Data sovereignty: the record stays in Lao PDR until the family authorizes someone to open it.

J4.1 — Crossing back: a Thai doctor sees the full history (Chiang Rai, 12 months later)

Precondition, as in J3: the DID documents are already loaded. The clinic viewer’s trust store is a cached copy of the GDHCN trust list, including Country B’s DSC DID document (STT-TRU-001, STT-TRU-002, STT-TRU-005). The clinic refreshes it nightly or hourly, against about weekly at the offline J3 clinic. It does not need to be online to verify: verifying reads the cache and never fetches the list. A DSC published after the last refresh fails the trust-list check (STT-VER-006), naming that reason.

Back where it began, Dr Anan sees Somsack at a primary care clinic in Chiang Rai. Bounmy shows the QR on his phone and says the passcode. The clinic viewer and the Lao gateway take five steps:

  1. The viewer verifies the HCERT against Country B’s DSC in its copy of the trust list.
  2. It requests the manifest by ITI-YY5, signed with HTTP Message Signatures (STT-VHL-005, STT-VHL-006, STT-VHL-008).
  3. The Lao gateway checks the passcode, the expiry and the consent, returns the manifest, and logs the access at home (TrustDemoJ41Access).
  4. The viewer decrypts the IPS and DVCs (STT-VHL-007) (TrustDemoJ41Received).
  5. It shows them to Dr Anan: every dose since the birth, viewed, never copied.

A wrong passcode is refused, and the attempt is still logged in Lao PDR (TrustDemoJ41WrongPasscode). If the HCERT does not verify (for example, Country B’s DSC is missing from the cached list), there is no digital verification. The clinic names the step and the reason (STT-VER-008), does not follow the VHL, and Dr Anan treats whatever the family can show as he would a paper record.

  • Pain point: Without the history, the doctor would repeat vaccines or have to guess.
  • Enabler: The record is fetched live from Lao PDR, viewed without being copied, and an access log is sent home.
  • Policy: Continuity of care across borders: each country keeps its own records, with no international patient registry and no vendor lock-in. What is agreed regionally is the data model and the trust list, not a shared copy of the data.

Questions for discussion

The VaxLink walkthrough closes with four policy questions:

  1. Identity for babies born abroad (J1, J2b): how should national immunization systems handle temporary ids for cross-border infants before civil registration is complete?
  2. Where trust begins (J2a, J3a): why should offline CHW devices store foreign cards as reported, not verified, rather than verify them in the field?
  3. Matching vaccines across countries (J3b): when a monovalent vaccine is given abroad but the home schedule uses a combination (pentavalent), how should schedule rules count the doses still due?
  4. Cross-border access and audit (J4.0b, J4.1): what agreements do countries need so that a clinician viewing a record abroad triggers an audit entry in the patient’s home country?
